# Reality Check

How to fill the odds, cost and fallback columns without either crushing the student or misleading them.

This is the part of the skill that does not exist anywhere else in the student's life. Everyone around them is either selling something or being kind. Be neither.

## The four numbers, every time

Every option in the table gets all four. An option that cannot be costed on all four has not been thought through.

**1. Time to first income.** From today until the first salary. Include the degree, the preparation, the notice, the licensing. This number is what makes an "obviously better" path visibly worse for a student who needs to support a family in eighteen months.

**2. Total money cost.** Fees plus living plus coaching plus the income not earned while studying. Foregone income is the number families almost never count, and it usually dominates.

**3. Realistic odds.** Not the topper's odds — this student's odds, given the marks and preparation actually in front of you. Where a selection ratio is publicly known, use it. Where it is not, describe the shape honestly: "roughly one in a hundred applicants", "most candidates who complete the coursework do get placed, but starting salaries vary widely by institution tier".

**4. What you hold if it fails.** The critical column. Some paths leave you with a degree, a certification and two years of relevant experience. Others leave you with nothing but time spent. That difference should drive the recommendation more than the size of the prize.

## Calibrating language to odds

Match the words to the number so the student cannot misread the register:

| Rough odds | How to say it |
|---|---|
| Most who try, succeed | "This is a matter of doing the work, not of getting lucky." |
| Roughly half | "A real contest. Plan for the possibility that it does not work." |
| Around 1 in 10 | "Long odds. Only worth it with a genuine backup running in parallel." |
| 1 in 100 or worse | "Very few people who start this finish it. That is not a reason not to try — it is a reason to have decided in advance what you do when it does not work, and when you will stop." |

Never write "just work hard and you will make it" for anything below the top row. It is the most common sentence in Indian career advice and the least true.

## Opportunity cost, made concrete

Compare like this rather than in the abstract:

> Two years preparing full-time for this exam: if it works, you start at [role] with a stable salary and a pension. If it does not — and for roughly nine in ten candidates it does not — you are 24 with no work experience, competing for entry-level roles against 22-year-olds. The same two years spent working plus preparing part-time gives you a lower chance at the exam and a much better position if the exam does not happen.

That paragraph is the single most useful thing this skill produces. Write a version of it for any long-shot, full-time-commitment path.

## Fallback ladder rules

- Every plan has at least a Plan B; long-odds plans have a Plan C.
- Each fallback has a **trigger**: a date or a result, not a feeling. "If I don't clear prelims by the second attempt" is a trigger. "If it isn't working out" is not.
- Each fallback has a **first action** for the week the trigger fires, so that the week is not lost to shock.
- The fallback must be secured *before* the risky path begins where possible — take the admission, keep the job offer, register for the backup exam. A fallback that has to be built after failure is not a fallback.

## Sanity checks before you deliver

- Have you ruled anything out, and given the reason? A plan with no eliminations has not narrowed anything.
- Does the recommendation survive the student's money constraint, or did that quietly get dropped?
- Is there a dated first action for this week?
- Would this document still be useful if the primary path fails? If not, the backup ladder is too thin.
- Did you soften any odds to make it feel nicer? Put it back.

## Where you must not go

- No prediction of a specific rank, cutoff, admission or salary outcome for this individual.
- No naming of coaching institutes, consultants or paid services as recommendations.
- No comparison with a named peer or sibling.
- No implication that a vocational, diploma, state-college or AYUSH route is a lesser life. For a large share of students these are the correct answer, and the tone of this section is often what decides whether they consider it.
